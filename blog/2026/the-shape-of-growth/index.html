The Shape of Growth: Tracing African Urbanisation

Created on November 11, 2026

2026   ·   urbanisation   africa   remote-sensing   ghsl   interactive   ·   data-stories

Most people outside the continent imagine African life as rural life. That image is inaccurate and outdated, and becoming more so every year. Africa was the last continent to urbanise, but it is now doing so at breakneck speed, faster than any other. Today nearly three-quarters of Africans live in urban or semi-urban settings.1 Over the next three decades Africa’s urban population will double, from about 700 million to 1.4 billion with two out of three Africans living in urban areas by 2050, making it the continent with the second-largest urban population after Asia.1 2.

Is this good news? Well, it depends. City life can improve people’s lives materially, change social and political attitudes,3 and make a less carbon-intensive lifestyle possible.4 But whether it does all that depends on the type of urbanisation. So let’s take a closer look at the shape of this remarkable growth.

Motivated by this great piece in the Financial Times and World Cities Day, I tracked the growth of built-up land and population in Africa’s 100 largest cities between 1990 and 2025, using data from the Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL), Open Street Map (OSM) and nighttime lights data captured by satelittes.5 6 For each city I hold its boundary fixed at its 2020 Africapolis outline and measure what happens inside it over time. The sample includes megacities like Lagos, Cairo and Nairobi, but also places like Osogbo, Mwanza and Bafoussam: cities of more than a million people that many outside the continent will never have heard of. Here I only look at the 100 largest cities but it is crucial to keep in mind, that beyond the largest cities, African urbanisation is also characterised by the explosive growth of small towns. Africa has over 1,400 intermediary cities that house over 36 percent of Africa’s total urban population.7

What the data show

Between 1990 and 2025, the built-up area of Africa’s 100 largest cities grew from about 6,900 to 12,500 km², an increase of 81%. Over the same period their population went from 142 to 370 million, an increase of 161%. By 2025, 89 of these cities had more than a million inhabitants, twice as many million-plus cities as Latin America. The chart below puts each city’s population and built-up land on the same scale, both starting at 100 in 1990. Where the people line (blue) ends above the built-up land line (orange), construction did not keep pace with population growth.

In 74 of Africa's 100 largest cities, population grew faster than built-up land.

So in most of the cities in our sample, 74 of the 100, population has grown faster than urban land. There are exceptions. Kigali, a city that has invested heavily in planned expansion, roads and serviced land under its citywide master plan, is one example. Juba, the capital of South Sudan, is a tragic one, where the civil war has caused major displacement. But in the median city, each million residents now live on 35 km² of built-up land, down from 48 km² in 1990. Within their boundaries, African cities have become markedly denser. But denser does not mean compact. More people per square kilometre of built-up land tells us nothing about where the new buildings went. As we will see, even inside the boundaries most new construction went onto empty land at the edge rather than filling in existing neighbourhoods. And beyond their boundaries, cities have spread further out creating peri-urban zones and satelitte towns.

The promise of density

Economic historians and political scientists have long pointed out that Africa has historically been held back by its relatively low population densities.8 9 Ask the economic consultants and they will point to the growth opportunities that come with a growing population. More people, more demand, more growth is the simplified logic. Beyond pure demand, should you ever enter a seminar room full of economists (especially the urban and economic-geography types), make sure “agglomeration economies” is on your bingo card. The idea has a long history in the discipline, running all the way back to Alfred Marshall’s Principles of Economics, published in 1890.10 Firms that cluster in cities gain access to a larger labour market, to specialised inputs and to more potential trading partners, and they benefit from knowledge spillovers: Marshall’s “ideas in the air”. Jane Jacobs later added that it is the sheer variety of activity in cities, not only the concentration of a single industry, that breeds new ideas.11 These productivity gains show up in the income gap between cities and the countryside,12 and the urban wage premium in low- and middle-income countries holds up under careful testing.13 The “growth miracles” of East Asia are unimaginable without urbanisation: in China, the urban population grew by 700 million people between 1978 and 2009, while GDP per person rose thirteen-fold.14

Denser, but spreading out

African cities do produce some of these gains, if modest ones: across 2,600 cities in 34 countries, a 10% increase in population density raises productivity by 0.2–0.5%, with most estimates at the lower end.2 But these gains depend on good spatial connectivity: workers need to be able to reach jobs, and firms each other. And even inside their boundaries, cities did not grow mainly by filling in. The GHSL data let us distinguish new construction on land that was already partly built up (“infill”) from construction on previously empty, greenfield land. Taking all 100 cities together, around 58% of new construction since 1990 went onto greenfield land. Greenfield construction is a lot more common in East (68%) and West Africa (60%) than in Southern (42%) and North African cities (47%), and the same ranking holds for the typical city in each region, shown below.

Greenfield does not mean empty. Land at the edge of a city is usually already doing something: growing food for the city, or holding trees and bush that shade it, soak up heavy rain and store carbon. So what did all this new construction replace? Matching each newly built pixel to a 1990 land-cover map gives a rough answer.

Across all cities, almost half of the new greenfield construction went onto tree cover, mostly tree-rich farmland and bush rather than forest, and about a quarter onto cropland. The mix varies by region. In West Africa, two-thirds replaced tree cover. In North Africa, where farmland is scarce, nearly half came out of cropland and most of the rest out of desert. This has implications for cities resilience to ever more frequent climate shocks since forests and grassland provide important ecosystem services to cities. Cutting trees contributes to urban heat island effects with deleterious effects on city-dwellers. Building on farmland can aggravate food insecurity in urban areas.

Building height fell in 99 of the 100 cities. Not one got taller.

As a result of this outward sprawl, average building height across African cities has actually decreased, not increased: the median city’s mean building height slipped from 7.7 m to 7.3 m between 2000 and 2025. In fact, not one city saw an increase. Height fell in 99 of the 100 cities and stayed flat in Lomé.15 Sprawl of this kind complicates infrastructure provision, and as a result many African cities are spatially dispersed and lack connectivity, leaving residents cut off from jobs and basic services such water, sanitation and waste management.12 One World Bank study finds the average African city about 20% more fragmented than cities in Asia, with 37% less exposure to people and jobs.16 What is more, African cities are, perhaps surprisingly, expensive cities. Households face costs about 29% higher than in other countries at similar income levels,16 and housing in sub-Saharan Africa is about 55% more expensive than in other low-income countries.17

Satellite towns and peri-urbanisation

And all of these numbers count only what happens inside each city’s fixed boundary. Step outside it and the sprawl is larger still: cities are consuming an ever-larger footprint of land, much of it beyond their official limits, creating mushrooming peri-urban zones and satelitte towns with poor connectivity and infrastructure access. The animations below show seven examples: Addis Ababa, Ouagadougou and Kigali growing outwards in rings, Cairo adding new towns in the desert, Lagos spreading inland from the coast, Kinshasa pushing east and south-west from a core that was already built up by 1990, and Johannesburg filling in and spreading across a sprawling, many-centred region. In Addis Ababa, roughly 140 km² of new built-up land lies just outside the city’s official boundary, about two-thirds as much again as the growth inside it.

A rough test with OpenStreetMap data points the same way. In seven cities where the map coverage is good enough to try (Lagos, Kinshasa, Kampala, Dar es Salaam, Khartoum, Juba and Mogadishu), residents of newly built areas at the edge live farther from the nearest mapped school, clinic or water point than residents of areas that densified: about 20% farther in Lagos, around 60% in Kampala and Mogadishu, and more than twice as far in Kinshasa.18

Urbanisation without industrialisation

Moreover, African urbanisation appears to be driven by different forces. Elsewhere, people moved to cities as economies shifted from farming to manufacturing. In Africa, however, urbanisation seems to follow different rules: it is less driven by this kind of structural transformation. Many African countries have urbanised with little growth in manufacturing,14 and unlike China, Africa has urbanised without major increases in agricultural or industrial productivity.12 Resource-rich countries in particular have produced what Gollin, Jedwab and Vollrath call “consumption cities”, where most jobs are in local trade and services rather than in productive, internationally competitive sectors.19 In fact, much of the growth is not driven by migration at all. Today, most of the increase in Africa’s urban population comes from births exceeding deaths in the cities themselves, rather than from people moving in from the countryside.20 21 22 Cities are growing whether or not the jobs are there. Without the jobs that industrialisation brings, the benefits of density are harder to realise, and the costs of poorly connected sprawl are harder to bear.

The infrastructure gap

Some basic services appear to be catching up. Nighttime-lights satellite data, admittedly not a perfect proxy, suggests that electrification is spreading fast in most African cities: the dark-or-dim share of built-up area fell from 92% to 5% in Mogadishu, 69% to 2% in Ikorodu, and 82% to 2% in Mwanza between 2000 and 2025, largely in the same fast-growing secondary cities.

Lights are not the whole story, though. In Afrobarometer’s latest surveys across 39 countries, almost all urban neighbourhoods (94%) had an electricity grid, but only 65% of urban residents said their power works most or all of the time.23 Only about half of urban neighbourhoods had a sewage system, half of urban households had no toilet inside the home, and one in five urban residents had repeatedly gone without enough clean water in the past year.24

In Onitsha, 87% of built-up land is still dark or barely lit at night.

These numbers are indicative and show that a substantial infrastructure gap remains. Where it persists, congestion and pollution rather than ideas will float the air. In 2020, African countries committed only 3.4% of GDP to infrastructure from all sources, about half the rate in South Asia, and more than 30% of what governments budget for infrastructure typically goes unspent.2 Public investment amounts to around USD 104 per person a year (in PPP), against a global average of USD 822.2. And the pressure is likely to grow. Fast growing cities face the highest risks of fragility characterised by inequality, youth unemployment and criminal violence25. What is more, Africa is warming slightly faster than the global average, by about 0.3 °C per decade since 1991, and fastest in the north.26 Many of its largest cities sit on the coast or on river floodplains. The number of Africans exposed to sea-level rise is projected to double from 54 million in 2000 to 108–116 million by 2030, and to reach 190–245 million by 2060; Lagos, Dar es Salaam, Cotonou and Alexandria are among the most exposed.27.

At the same time, much of the continent’s urban fabric does not exist yet: by one estimate, two-thirds of Africa’s cities are yet to be built.13 That makes the choices made now unusually consequential. Cities in developing countries are among the places most exposed to climate change, but they are also the most viable way of managing its impacts.4 Density cuts emissions per person from infrastructure and services, and taller buildings are less carbon-intensive, which makes the fall in building height described above all the more worrying. Access to local public goods and services also helps households cope with environmental shocks. And what gets built today will last: urban infrastructure typically stays in use for around 125 years and buildings for around 175, so today’s decisions about a city’s physical shape will lock in its costs and emissions for generations.4

What can be done

So, what can be done? A whole suite of recent reports provide answers and insights, in the following I draw heavily on them.28 29 30 For starters, more money would not hurt. Africa’s need for infrastructure investment is estimated at USD 130 - USD 170 billion annually, currently only half of it is committed.29 Africa urbanises rapidly at historically low income levels, this means that most countries lack the necessary means to make the required investments. As things stand, city governments are poorly placed to close the gap. Municipalities are cash-strapped, with low tax revenues, and they often lack the political discretion and financial autonomy to raise money on financial markets.31

In a first instance, municipalities should strengthen efforts to broaden their revenue base. Most African cities operate obsolete property valuation methods inherited from the colonial era.32 In 2018, under leadership of Yvonne Aki-Sawyerr, Freetown introduced a property tax reform which adopted a simple points-based valuation method to reevaluate properties. After initial resistance from the central government, the reform doubled the register, nearly tripled tax collections and introduced progressive taxation, with around 70% of the increased potential revenue coming from the top quarter of properties (https://www.theigc.org/blogs/reforming-property-tax-valuation-sierra-leone). The model is now being adopted in other cities across Sierra Leone.

A broadened revenue base should also facilitate access to other sources of finance and enable cities to borrow. As Haas and her colleagues show, in most countries, legal frameworks allow for some form of long-term subnational borrowing. And yet, nearly half of these countries have seen no sub-national borrowing activity at all. Where municipal borrowing has occurred, it has been minimal, typically involving just a single city attempting to access the market once.29 33 One reason might be that in all but four countries for which data was available, subnational borrowing requires approval from a central government authority. At the same time, fiscal transfers from central governments remain crucial but are often too small in volume and their timings too erratic, creating uncertainty for local governments.29 Here, Mexico’s advances in fiscal devolution and transfer reform in the early 2000s provide important lessons.The reforms allow local governments to leverage predictable intergovernmental transfers as a stable revenue stream against which to borrow and enabled municipalities to raise over USD 4 billion in local currency for infrastructure investment, using domestic capital markets.29

But, money isn’t everything. Strikingly, many African cities appear to struggle to spend even the limited financing they already have access too. For example, Kisumu, Kenya was only able to spend 32% of its allocated capital investment budget in the financial year 2019/20, and this was without taking on further finance from capital markets.29 Due to a mix of a lack of integrated infrastructure plans, capacity constraints and issues with procurement processes, many cities find it difficult to deliver a pipeline of projects which are both, bankable and address their most important constraints. In the best cases, these plans are embedded within a compelling narrative showing how these projects contribute to the sustainable development of the city. Linking the infrastructure gap specifically to the need for projects that contribute to the economic, social and environmental progress of its inhabitants opens further avenues for tapping into new sources of financing from ESG-minded investors. After Johannesburg and Cape Town, the city of Tanga’s water utility in Tanzania and Lagos state have issued green bonds to finance investments in water provision and drainage, renewable energy generation and waste management. Another interesting space to watch are urban bature-based solutions (nbs) that address both, mitigation and adaptation. In urban contexts, NbS are particularly valued for their potential to support sustainable development while enhancing the health and well-being of city dwellers.34

Dakar is a case in point: in 2015 the city’s planned CFA 20 billion (about €30 million) municipal bond, backed by a USAID guarantee and a credit rating, was blocked at the last minute by the national government, amid a long-running political conflict between the city and the state.2 35 36 There are signs of change. In 2025 the African Development Bank lent Johannesburg ZAR 2.5 billion (about US$139 million), its first loan to an African city without a national government guarantee.37

Change will take time. In the meantime, African entrepreneurs are already busy finding solutions to pressing infrastructure challenges. Infrahub.africa hosts a collection of 73 initiatives from 27 African countries providing solutions for waste collection and management, sanitation, mobility and many more. Data and caveats

Built-up area and population come from the Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL BUILT-S and GHS-POP, release R2023A, European Commission JRC), in five-year epochs from 1990 to 2025. Values between epochs are interpolated linearly for the animation. City boundaries are Africapolis 2020 agglomerations (OECD/SWAC), held fixed at their 2020 extent for all years. Building height is GHSL BUILT-V (volume) divided by BUILT-S (footprint), available 2000–2025. Nighttime-lights figures use a bloom- and top-coding-corrected DMSP series, also read from 2000. In the footprint animations, an area counts as built up if at least 10% of it is covered by buildings.

Two caveats are worth keeping in mind. Because the boundaries are fixed, growth that spills past them is not counted, so land growth is understated for cities that have outgrown their mapped area. Kinshasa and Luanda had already spread beyond theirs by 1990, and Addis Ababa has since (see above). Africapolis defines an agglomeration physically, as at least 10,000 people in a continuous built-up area with less than 200 metres between buildings, so scattered construction beyond that distance falls outside the boundary by design. And for several smaller cities, the large percentage changes partly reflect how the boundary polygon was drawn rather than actual growth.
